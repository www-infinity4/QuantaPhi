Fusion research receives 28 new project selections, not a finished power plant

By QuantaPhi Research Desk · November 2, 2026 · Source published November 2, 2026

The Energy Department announced $8.5 million in proposed INFUSE research awards; the projects must still negotiate funding and deliver technical results.

Fusion energy took another incremental research step on November 2 when the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Science announced 28 project selections under the Innovation Network for Fusion Energy program. The proposed support totals $8.5 million for fiscal year 2026. That is a meaningful public research investment, but it is not evidence that a commercial fusion station has begun generating electricity.

The program, known as INFUSE, brings private fusion developers together with national laboratories and universities. Instead of every company building and maintaining all its own research equipment, participants can work with established scientific facilities and specialists. The objective is to reduce specific technical uncertainties that affect the path toward practical fusion systems. Different teams may work on different bottlenecks rather than produce identical devices.

The department says 17 projects are led by national laboratories and 11 by universities, with 22 separate commercial enterprises involved. The selected research covers modeling and simulation, materials, plasma measurements, and supporting technologies. These are foundational areas. They influence whether a system can maintain suitable operating conditions, withstand stress, and provide measurements that engineers can trust.

Fusion joins light atomic nuclei under extreme conditions, releasing energy. The headline idea is attractive, but doing this usefully requires far more than a laboratory demonstration. A future plant would need to control its plasma, handle heat, maintain components, capture energy, and operate economically. Improving a diagnostic instrument or a material may contribute to that goal without solving all the other pieces.

According to the announcement, individual project budgets range from $100,000 to $500,000. The selected work lasts one or two years. These numbers help explain what the announcement represents: targeted technical projects rather than the entire cost of developing or constructing a power station. A meaningful assessment should look at the research questions, experimental milestones, and actual results delivered by the funded teams.

The Energy Department also described a fiscal year 2027 request for assistance with expanded participation opportunities for consortia and a topic involving early concepts for fusion pilot plants. The existence of that new application process is a signal of planning activity, not proof that any particular pilot facility is funded or built. Researchers and companies will still need to make and test the underlying engineering claims.

One especially important caution appears in DOE's own announcement. The selections are for award negotiations. The agency says they do not guarantee that funding will be issued; negotiations can be canceled and a selection rescinded. Reporting the $8.5 million as money already paid to all recipients would go beyond the agency's wording. Readers should distinguish an announced selection from an executed grant or contract.

What should an interested reader watch next? First, whether the negotiations finish and final awards are made. Second, whether teams publish credible test results on their stated research questions. Third, whether successful experiments show that their innovations work in integrated systems and under realistic conditions. Each milestone offers better evidence than a headline alone.

Fusion may eventually become a powerful energy resource, but the route is a sequence of experimentally verified improvements. This announcement concerns public-private research intended to move that process forward. It should be understood in its actual scale and timing rather than used to claim an imminent commercial breakthrough.
