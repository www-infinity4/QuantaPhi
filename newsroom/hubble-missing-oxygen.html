Why Hubble's missing oxygen matters to the history of small galaxies

By QuantaPhi Research Desk · November 2, 2026 · Source published November 2, 2026

A study of 37 nearby dwarf galaxies finds less oxygen than models predict and raises new questions about the powerful winds that carry elements into space.

New results from the Hubble Space Telescope have put an old question about galaxy evolution under sharper scrutiny: how much of the material created by stars stays in the galaxy where those stars formed? Researchers examining 37 nearby dwarf galaxies found a deficit of oxygen compared with what models predict. The result is not a finding that oxygen has somehow vanished. It points to gas being moved out of small galaxies, potentially by energetic processes linked to massive stars.

The project is called Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds, or GLOW. Scientists combined observations of individual stars with measurements of the material between stars to reconstruct a history of oxygen production. Oxygen is especially useful for this kind of investigation because stars make it, astronomers can detect it comparatively well, and its production helps trace the history of heavier chemical elements. The measured balance becomes a test of whether galaxy simulations account for the pathways that material takes.

Why do dwarf galaxies make an interesting test? Their gravitational pull is comparatively weak. Energy from large stars and explosions at the end of stellar lives can push gas out of these systems more readily than out of a large galaxy. A supernova does more than brighten the sky for a brief interval: the released energy can help generate expanding gas flows, commonly called galactic winds. Those winds can redistribute material that future generations of stars might otherwise use.

According to NASA's November 2 account of the new research, the amount of oxygen retained in these small galaxies is considerably below what some current simulations suggest. That difference is important because a good model needs to reproduce the real chemical inventory, not just the rough size or brightness of a galaxy. An apparent shortage can reveal assumptions about stellar feedback, gas movement, or the way such activity changes across galaxies of different masses.

To make the comparison, the researchers used archived optical images from Hubble to examine resolved star populations. They also worked with information from the retired Spitzer Space Telescope, the Very Large Array, and the Australian Telescope Compact Array. These sources observe different parts of the physical story. Taken together, they allow investigators to build a more detailed picture than a single telescope could provide.

There is still an important uncertainty. Researchers can infer that a large share of oxygen left the regions where it formed, but it is harder to say precisely where every portion ended up. Some may lie in diffuse gas surrounding the galaxy. Some could have traveled farther into the space between galaxies. Measuring that destination requires other observations; the oxygen deficit alone does not map the material's full journey.

The finding also connects a small galaxy to a much larger question. Heavy elements created by stars eventually become raw materials for later stars, planets, and complex chemistry. If dwarf galaxies transfer those elements outward more efficiently than expected, the history of enrichment outside galaxies may need adjustment. The conclusion should be tied to the galaxies actually observed, rather than assuming all galaxies behave identically.

Researchers plan follow-up work. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is expected to help identify even smaller nearby galaxies over wider areas of the sky, while detailed observations from Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope can characterize stars and past star formation. Those investigations can test whether the same patterns extend to systems that the initial sample could not resolve.

The key point for readers is the difference between an observed mismatch and a solved mechanism. The observation is that the sampled dwarf galaxies show less oxygen than expected. Energetic stellar winds offer a physical explanation and an avenue for testing models. Exactly how much material reached each surrounding region remains a topic for additional measurements. This is a new result published November 2, not an update or redating of the older Hubble servicing story.
